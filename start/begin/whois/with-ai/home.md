## Basic Intro to WhoIs

WhoIs is a public section of a website where a person, team or organisation explains who they are, what they value, how they work and what they are trying to become. Its backing Git repository is public as well, so there is a visible history of **who said what and when**.

It is not only a biography. A useful WhoIs can include:

- An introduction to the people and purpose behind the work.
- Values, ethics and ways of working.
- Qualitative pledges to causes, communities or forms of service.
- Wishes, hopes, questions and observations about the world.
- Authorship, ownership and collaboration terms.
- Remuneration, hosting and expectations around technical or mentoring labour.
- An evolving record of decisions, learning, contradictions and course correction.

The archive shows WhoIs as a living context around a website, rather than a static profile page polished once and then abandoned.

## Lack of Visibility in the Internet Today

Much of the internet makes it difficult to know who is behind a project, what they intended at the beginning, how their position changed or who is responsible for the work. A public brand can appear highly finished while its ownership, motives, labour and decision-making remain hidden.

Social feeds also separate statements from their original context. A post may be shared widely without the surrounding values, terms, corrections or history. The result is a culture of strong impressions with very little durable accountability.

WhoIs responds by putting context near the work. It asks people and organisations to explain their intentions, name their responsibilities and leave enough history for others to understand how a project evolved.

## Everyone is Polishing and Performing

Online identity is often treated as performance. People polish portfolios, testimonials and social profiles while hiding uncertainty, failed experiments, changing beliefs and difficult decisions. Organisations publish values statements that are not connected to their daily behaviour.

WhoIs makes room for a fuller picture. It can include aspiration without pretending that aspiration is achievement. It can acknowledge limitations, describe a trial period, record unfinished thinking and show how a person or team is trying to improve.

The intention is not to demand embarrassing exposure. It is to replace the false completeness of polished presentation with **honest, bounded and evolving context**.

## No Open Record of Intention and Performance

Most websites do not preserve a clear record of what their creators said they would do, what standards they adopted or how they responded when reality disagreed with the plan. Without that record, it is difficult to distinguish a genuine change of direction from quiet abandonment, or a mistake from deliberate misrepresentation.

A WhoIs section can hold the initial intention, the working assumptions, the pledges and the later observations. Git adds a technical history to that human record. Together they make it possible to ask:

- What was promised or proposed?
- What was actually attempted?
- What changed, and why?
- Who was affected?
- What was learned?
- What remains unresolved?

This is a qualitative record, not a mechanical scorecard. The aim is understanding and responsibility, not reducing a person to performance metrics.

## We Are Not Here to Judge

WhoIs is not intended to create a court of public opinion. People are allowed to grow, change their minds, make mistakes and hold different beliefs. The 2025 material explicitly leaves room for pluralism, including atheists and agnostics, and treats disagreement as an opportunity for checks and balances rather than automatic exclusion.

Transparency must not become surveillance, gossip or moral grandstanding. A person should be able to state what is private, what is public and what is still being worked through. Sensitive information should not be published merely because openness is fashionable.

The standard is good faith: make meaningful intentions visible, do not knowingly mislead, respond to serious contradictions and treat people with dignity.

## Yet We Want Transparency

Not judging does not mean refusing to see. The network wants enough transparency for people to make informed choices about collaboration, money, authorship, access and trust.

That includes declaring the ethical frame of the work, identifying the author and architect, explaining proprietary or licensed elements, making ownership of content clear and stating how remuneration works. It also includes saying when the relationship is a mutual trial and what would happen if the parties no longer align.

Transparency is strongest when it is reciprocal. Leaders should publish their terms and limitations, while collaborators should be able to ask questions, raise red flags and record their own understanding of the agreement.

## Rewarding Organization Members and Preventing Hiding Behind Bureaucracy

An organisation can hide responsibility behind titles, departments, policies and anonymous processes. WhoIs puts human context back into the system. It lets members describe their roles, values, contributions and working relationships rather than allowing a logo or bureaucracy to speak for everyone.

The archive also insists that labour has value. Technical architecture, content work, mentoring, hosting and continued support should be remunerated fairly and discussed directly. A public statement of terms helps prevent the fiction that valuable work is free simply because a platform hides its costs through data extraction or unpaid labour.

Recognition does not require celebrity. It means naming contributions, honouring authorship, clarifying responsibilities and sharing abundance where the work has benefited from the wider team.

## Why A Public Evolving Record of Who a Person / Team is and How an Organization Evolves in Important

A public evolving record gives a project memory. It shows the person or team behind the work, the causes they care about, the constraints they face, the commitments they have made and the lessons they have gathered.

For an organisation, this record can connect:

- Identity and purpose.
- Values and everyday decisions.
- Pledges and evidence of effort.
- Compensation and contribution.
- Public work and private boundaries.
- Disagreement and repair.
- Early experiments and later direction.

This matters because organisations are not only structures. They are relationships that evolve over time. A WhoIs record lets future members understand how the culture was formed instead of inheriting unexplained rules and folklore.

## A Commitment to Explore This On Day 1 and the Become Progressively Transparent

WhoIs should begin on day one, even if the first version is short. A new person or team can state who they are, what they are exploring, what they hope to contribute, what support they need and what they do not yet know.

Progressive transparency means adding detail as trust, experience and responsibility grow. The record can develop from an introduction into values, ways of working, pledges, observations, decisions, public learnings and carefully considered responses to difficulty.

DAWN treats this as part of joining rather than an optional marketing exercise. Prospective participants are asked to consider their worldview, their relationship to the mission, their willingness to learn the platform, their support for the network and their approach to parting ways if necessary.

## Whistleblowing and Contradictions

An open record gives people somewhere to raise a contradiction when a public claim and observable practice do not match. It can support whistleblowing by preserving context, authorship and a traceable record instead of forcing concerns into rumours or disposable private messages.

Whistleblowing still requires care. Serious claims should be specific, made in good faith and separated from speculation or personal retaliation. Where safety, privacy or legal obligations require it, a concern may need to begin through a protected private channel before any public record is made.

Contradictions are not automatically proof of bad character. They may reveal changed circumstances, incomplete work, a misunderstanding or a genuine breach of trust. The WhoIs approach is to document the issue, invite a response, correct the record where possible and preserve the history of what was learned.

## A New Humanity Built on Trust

Trust is not blind optimism. It is confidence built through visible intentions, consistent action, honest correction and fair treatment. A public WhoIs record gives trust something to rest on without pretending that people are static or infallible.

The wider hope is a new humanity that can collaborate without hiding behind platforms, departments or carefully managed personas. People can own their content, name their labour, share their values, challenge each other and continue learning together.

This is the social purpose behind the technical arrangement: public files and public Git histories can support a culture of accountability, goodwill and shared abundance when the people using them also practise those qualities.

## Takeaways on WhoIs Adoption

- Start with a clear introduction to the person, team or organisation.
- State values, ethics, ways of working and qualitative pledges.
- Explain ownership, authorship, remuneration and technical boundaries.
- Record wishes, observations, uncertainties and changes of direction.
- Keep the backing Git repository public where the content is public.
- Use history to understand decisions, not to manufacture shame.
- Make private and confidential boundaries explicit.
- Invite questions, contradictions and good-faith correction.
- Review and update the record as the person, team or organisation evolves.
- Treat WhoIs as a shared practice of trust, not a branding exercise.

## In Conclusion

WhoIs is a public, evolving account of intention and responsibility. It tells visitors who is speaking, what they value, how they work, what they are trying to do and how their understanding changes over time.

The 2025 archive presents this as part of a broader experiment in transparent collaboration. Its promise is not that openness removes conflict. Its promise is that visible context, fair exchange and honest records can help people handle conflict with more maturity and build organisations that deserve trust.
